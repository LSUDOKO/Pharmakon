Keeping Uncertainty Visible: A Verified, Probabilistic Reading of Dioscorides’ De materia medica, from Greek Plant Names to Testable Hypotheses

Arpit, National Institute of Technology Hamirpur, India · Short paper · Code and data: doi:10.5281/zenodo.24106413

Keywords: Dioscorides; Byzantine medicine; ancient Greek; uncertainty; plant identification; ethnopharmacology; reproducibility; knowledge graphs

1. Introduction

The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognised the discovery of artemisinin, a lead its discoverer found in a fourth-century Chinese handbook (Tu 2011). Europe has a comparable source: Dioscorides’ De materia medica (first century CE), copied, translated and adapted for some fifteen centuries (Leonti 2011; Staub et al. 2016). Three constraints separate such a text from testable science. The identity of its plants is uncertain. The survival of its uses through later tradition has rarely been measured at scale. And the claim that historical use anticipates pharmacological activity is seldom tested in a way that excludes hindsight (Buenz et al. 2004).

This paper presents Pharmakon, an open pipeline that treats these constraints not as noise to be cleaned away but as information to be modelled and shown. Every output carries its uncertainty, and every claim leads back to the exact Greek words that state it. It is built for three readers at once: historians, who can read Dioscorides with evidence attached to each use; laboratories, who receive a shortlist with reasons; and conservation, since every species carries its conservation status where one has been assessed.

2. Background

Machine learning for ancient languages has advanced restoration, attribution and annotation (Assael et al. 2022; Sommerschield et al. 2023), and open TEI editions with CTS citation, such as Open Greek and Latin’s First1KGreek corpus, now make Greek technical literature machine-readable. In ethnopharmacology, Staub et al. (2016) quantified 5,314 therapeutic uses of 536 plant taxa in a Renaissance edition of Dioscorides, and Lardos et al. (2024) proposed a systematic, expert-driven method for assessing plant identities in a thirteenth-century Byzantine text, John the Physician’s Therapeutics. Mining historical texts for leads has been advocated for two decades (Buenz et al. 2004), but such work usually commits to a single identification per name and rarely publishes a prospective test of its predictive value. Pharmakon joins these strands: philological verifiability from digital humanities, probabilistic identification informed by expert criteria, and open chemistry and disease resources (Rutz et al. 2022; Zdrazil et al. 2024; Buniello et al. 2025).

3. Sources and method

Texts. Wellmann’s edition of Dioscorides (1906–1914) and Galen’s De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis ac facultatibus (Kühn 1826) are taken from First1KGreek TEI (CC BY-SA 4.0) and segmented into 1,500 Dioscorides passages and 476 Galen drug entries, each with a CTS URN. John the Physician enters through the 61 names identified by Lardos et al. (2024), and the living tradition through 1,226 published ethnobotanical records from Mount Pelion and the North Aegean (Tsioutsiou et al. 2023; Axiotis et al. 2018).

Reading. A rule-based extractor, driven by a Greek condition lexicon, proposes medical-use claims. Each claim must store the substring that states it, and a Unicode-normalised check archives any claim whose quotation is not found in its passage: 3,696 claims passed with no mismatches. Of 158 Greek condition terms, 134 map to verified MeSH headings and 24 are kept as having no modern equivalent. Language models later proposed further claims and lexicon stems under the same verification rule; these are stored separately and excluded from every test reported below.

Identifying. For each ancient name, a conditional-logit model ranks GBIF-verified candidate species using geography, continuity with modern Greek names, agreement among scholarly identifications and genus consensus, with an explicit “unidentified” alternative. The output is a probability distribution with bootstrap intervals, not a label. Because the “unidentified” option has its own learned utility, a name with thin evidence receives a high probability of remaining unidentified instead of a forced answer.

Tracing. Each name–use pair is followed through Galen, John the Physician and the modern records. Galen entries that copy Dioscorides (word 4-gram containment above 0.15) are down-weighted, and observed survival is compared with a permutation null.

Testing. A rolling-origin backtest stands at 2000, 2005, 2010 and 2015, uses only information dated before each cutoff, and asks whether historical features predict which unstudied plant–condition pairs later acquire PubMed pharmacology co-mentions.

The results form a knowledge graph of 2,585 nodes and 9,146 edges, published through a website, a JSON API and a Model Context Protocol server, so that people and AI assistants query the same evidence.

4. Results

Identification. Holding out each expert name in turn, the model’s first choice matches the expert identification for 62% of the 61 names (95% CI 51–75%; Table 1, Figure 1). Baselines matter: candidate lists are short (141 candidates in total, from the same expert study), so a uniform guess already reaches 39% and a genus-frequency prior 38%, while the standard lexicon’s single identification reaches 31%. A transfer model using no counts derived from the expert study reaches 56%, the cleanest estimate. Calibration misses its target (expected calibration error 0.14–0.21 against ≤ 0.10).

Survival. Of 508 name–use rows whose plant is attested in the modern record, 48.6% recur, against 40.8% under 1,000 permutations (p = 0.001). The Mount Pelion study concerns skin ailments, so this sample favours skin uses.

Prediction. Historical features never improved on research popularity; at 2005 they made the ranking worse (Appendix, Table A1). We treat this null as a central finding.

Hypotheses. Weighting each untested pair by the probability of its plant yields 1,781 research hypotheses; ignoring identification uncertainty would change 5 of the top 20. Seven of the top 20 carry a toxicity flag, which is displayed rather than filtered.

5. Discussion

Pharmakon reads creativity under constraint as a methodological choice: the fragile parts of the evidence, namely contested names, conditions without modern equivalents and a disappointing prediction test, are displayed rather than resolved. Verbatim spans and CTS URNs keep every computed claim answerable to the text; the “unidentified” option lets the model decline to guess; the null result disciplines claims that ancient use “predicts” modern activity. Limitations remain. Expert judgement is not ground truth, a PubMed co-mention is only a proxy for evidence, extraction and condition mapping await blinded human audits, and the Byzantine chain still lacks Paul of Aegina. All outputs are research hypotheses, not medical advice.

For digital humanities, the project offers a template for computational reading in which every machine-made claim remains a citable passage, and in which a negative result is a publishable output rather than a failure. Next steps are blinded expert audits of extraction and identification, richer plant descriptions, further Byzantine authors, and a partnership with a pharmacognosy laboratory to test the highest-ranked non-toxic hypotheses. Code, data and a citable archive are open (Arpit 2026).
